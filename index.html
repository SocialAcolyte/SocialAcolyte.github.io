They gave it to the winner full of oil, pressed from the sa­cred olives of Athena that grew in the groves out­side the city, and a man who won the foot race or the wrestling might carry home enough of these jars to keep his family for a gen­er­a­tion. Every one was made to the same measure, as the law re­quired, and every one was paint­ed in the old way, black figures on the red of the clay, long after the potters of the Ker­a­mei­kos had learned to do the re­verse, because a prize should look like the priz­es of grand­fa­thers. On the far side the goddess strides out with her spear raised and her shield on her arm, be­tween two col­umns topped with cock­er­els. On this side men fight with their hands and their feet. It is the pank­ra­tion, the all-in fight, where any­thing was al­lowed except biting and gouging, and a judge in a long cloak stands over them with a forked rod, ready to bring it down on the one who cheats. One man has caught the other by the leg; the other has his fist drawn back. Nobody yet knows who will win. The clay was dug beside the river and left out to weath­er, then washed and set­tled and knead­ed until it was smooth, and thrown on the wheel in pieces, foot and body and neck, that were joined when they were leather-hard. The paint­er laid the figures on in a slip of the fin­est clay, which looks no dif­fer­ent from the rest until the kiln does its work: a fire with air, a fire starved of air, then air again, and the slip stays black and glossy while the body turns back to or­ange. Then he scratched the mus­cles and the folds of the cloak in with a needle, line by line, so that the red shows through like blood under skin. Across the city the ath­letes trained naked in the gym­na­si­um and oiled them­selves before and scraped them­selves clean after, and the oil came from the same trees. The win­ner's name was cried out in the sta­di­um and cut into stone, and he was given a crown of olive and these jars by the dozen, sealed with clay and stamped. Some were sold abroad, which was al­lowed, and went on ships to Etru­ria and the Black Sea, packed in straw, and were bur­ied with men who had never seen Athens. The oil was used long ago, for lamps and for cooking, for bread and for the bodies of the dead, and the win­ner's name has gone with it. What is left is the clay, which does not rot, and the black that the fire fixed in it, and the fight, still un­de­cid­ed: two men locked to­geth­er under the judge's raised rod for two and a half thou­sand years, wait­ing for some­one to fall.

At Jing­dez­hen the whole town lived by the colour of the smoke. There were so many kilns, and they were fired so often, that trav­el­lers said the place looked from the hills like a city under siege, and at night the sky over the river glowed red. The clay came down from the high ridge at Gao­ling, white as rice, and the stone that made it glassy came from an­oth­er hill; both were crushed by water wheels, washed, set­tled in pits and left to age in the dark until they were smooth as skin. No one made a pot alone. One man threw the gourd in two halves on a wheel turned by a boy, and an­oth­er joined them and trimmed the waist; one ground the cobalt, one drew the out­lines, one only filled them in, an­oth­er wrote the mark on the foot, and a single vase could pass through seventy pairs of hands before it was fired. The cobalt came from Persia at first, along the same roads as the horses, and later from the hills of Zhe­jiang. With a brush of rabbit hair the paint­er laid it over the raw body in vines and leaves and little hang­ing gourds, which wish a house full of sons, and bats among them, because the word for bat sounds like the word for luck. Noth­ing showed. The blue went on grey and dull as ash, and you had to trust your hand. Then the glaze, poured on, or blown on through a tube of bam­boo; then the pots set in sag­gars of coarse clay, stacked in col­umns to the roof of the kiln, and the door bricked up and the fire lit with pine. For three days and two nights the stok­ers fed it and watched the flame through a spy­hole, judg­ing by its colour, white with yellow at the heart, when the heat was enough to turn sand to glass. Then they let it die and waited for the bricks to cool. When the sag­gars were cracked open the colour had ar­rived, deep and wet as the sea at eve­ning, under a skin of ice that will never melt. The best went north on the Grand Canal to the palace; the rest went south to Canton, and from there in the holds of the Dutch ships to Am­ster­dam, packed in tea, where people who had never seen a gourd vine paid a for­tune for the picture of one and set it on a high shelf in a cold room by the sea. This one came to New York in the end. It has stood there through every winter since, and the bats still hang among the leaves, and the luck they prom­ise has not yet been used up.

Someone cut forty-five little stamps: a running man, a plumed head, a child, a fish, a bird in flight, a ship, a hide, a comb, a flower with eight petals. Then one day, in a palace above the plain of the Mesara, they took a disc of the finest clay while it was still soft, drew a spiral on it with a point, and began at the rim. Each sign went in under a thumb, one after another, and every word was closed with a short stroke across the line. They turned the disc as they worked, round and in, round and in, until they came to the middle and the clay was full; then they turned it over and filled the other side. Afterwards it was fired as hard as a tile, and kept. That was more than three and a half thousand years ago. It lay in the ruins of the palace until the summer of 1908, when an Italian archaeologist lifted it out of a basement room beside a broken tablet, and since then it has been read by everyone and by no one. Two hundred and forty-two signs in sixty-one words, and not one of them understood. It has been called a hymn to a mother goddess, a roll of soldiers, a calendar, a contract, a board game, and a fake. Nobody has found another page made with those stamps, or the stamps themselves, though they are the oldest movable type we know of, cut so that a hand could say the same thing again and again. Perhaps they only ever said this. But somebody once held it as you are holding this one, and turned it slowly, and the words came round to them one at a time under the thumb, and they knew exactly what it said.

And this too shall pass

A king, the story goes, asked his wise men for a ring that would make a sad man glad and a glad man sad. They argued for a year. Then the youngest went down to the market, to an old engraver who worked in a doorway with a lamp and a file, and came back with a plain band of bronze. The king turned it in his fingers and read what was cut round the edge, and was quiet for a long time. He wore it at his wedding and at his son's burial, at the signing of the peace and on the night the city burned, on his good days and on his bad ones, and it was true on all of them. When he was old he gave it to a beggar at the gate, who sold it for bread.

Cap. I. Ecclesiastes 117

The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem. Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity. What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?

One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever. The sun also ariseth, and the sun goeth down, and hasteth to his place where he arose. The wind goeth toward the south, and turneth about unto the north; it whirleth about continually, and the wind returneth again according to his circuits. All the rivers run into the sea; yet the sea is not full; unto the place from whence the rivers come, thither they return again.

All things are full of labour; man cannot utter it: the eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing. The thing that hath been, it is that which shall be; and that which is done is that which shall be done: and there is no new thing under the sun. Is there any thing whereof it may be said, See, this is new? it hath been already of old time, which was before us. There is no remembrance of former things; neither shall there be any remembrance of things that are to come with those that shall come after.

The words of the Preacher. The Hebrew name is Qoheleth, from qahal, to gather or call together: the speaker is one who assembles, whether listeners to hear him or sayings to keep. The Greek translators made him Ekklesiastes, a man of the assembly, and the Latin kept the Greek word for its title, though Jerome in his commentary called him Concionator, the one who addresses the gathering. The form is feminine, as the names of offices sometimes are in Hebrew, and the speaker gives it to himself only seven times. Tradition gives the book to Solomon in old age, as it gives him the Song in his youth and the Proverbs in his prime; the text itself never once says his name.

Vanity of vanities. The word is hevel, breath: the vapour seen on a cold morning, the warmth of a word in the air, gone before it can be held. Doubled, it becomes a superlative in the Hebrew manner, as the holy of holies is the holiest place and the song of songs the finest song; so this is the most breath of all breath. The Latin vanitas vanitatum made it emptiness, and the English followed; mist or smoke may stand closer, for the thing it names is not worthless but brief. Hevel is also the name of Abel, whose life is the shortest in Genesis. The word comes some thirty-eight times in these twelve chapters, and it opens and closes the book like a breath drawn and let go.

Under the sun. A phrase this book uses twenty-nine times and no other book of the Bible uses at all. It marks the field of the inquiry: what can be seen by daylight, in this life, by a man who will not argue from beyond it. Phoenician kings had the same words carved on their coffins, cursing whoever disturbed their bones to have no seed among the living under the sun, so the phrase was an old name for the world of the living before the Preacher took it up. Elsewhere he says under heaven, and means much the same.

Hasteth to his place. The verb is sha’af, to pant: the word for a runner out of breath, or for the wild ass in Jeremiah that snuffs up the wind. The sun does not glide back to the east by night but hurries there gasping, to rise again where it rose before; the picture is not of serenity but of labour without arrival. Some old interpreters heard eagerness in it, the sun longing for its place; most heard weariness, and the verses that follow give them reason.

The sea is not full. The old puzzle of the natural philosophers: every river runs down into the sea, and the sea rises no higher. Aristotle answered it in his Meteorologica with the sun’s heat drawing the water up as vapour, to fall again as rain, and many Christian readers took this verse to know the same thing. The Hebrew is barer: to the place where the rivers go, there they go again. The circle is the point, not the mechanism; nothing is gained by all that running, and nothing, at least, is lost.

Man cannot utter it. All things are full of labour; or, as the words may as well be read, all words are weary. The Hebrew devarim means both words and things, and the verse lets the reader hear both at once: things are too wearisome to tell, and words are worn out with the telling. The Preacher, whose trade is words, admits at the outset that they will not be adequate, and then goes on for twelve chapters, which is the honest practice of every writer.

See, this is new? The Preacher hears the objection before anyone makes it, and answers in the same breath: it hath been already of old time. What seems new is only unremembered, which is the matter of the verse that follows. Readers in every century have heard the question asked of their own century’s wonders, and have found it harder to answer than it looks.

King in Jerusalem. Solomon is never named, and the kingship is soon set aside: after the second chapter the speaker talks like a subject who watches kings from below, who has seen the tears of the oppressed and the poor wise man forgotten. Hence the old opinion, already in the Talmud, that the book was written down by Hezekiah and his company, the same men who copied out the proverbs of Solomon; and the newer one, that the royal name is a robe the author puts on to make his trial of pleasure and of wisdom with the largest means a man could have. Either way the robe is worn lightly. A king who has had everything is the right witness that everything is not enough.

One generation passeth away. The going of the first clause is the going of death, as we too say that a man has passed, or is gone; the coming of the second is birth. Between the two words lies a whole life, and the sentence does not stop for it. The generations follow one another as the rivers of the seventh verse follow one another to the sea, and with the same result.

The earth abideth for ever. The men go and the ground stays. One might expect the sentence to comfort: the field outlasts its ploughman, the house its builder, something remains. But it is set here as a reproach, for what abides is not the one who laboured. The stage is permanent and the players are not, and the earth stands only to watch the generations cross it, as a pavement is worn by feet it never remembers. Even its abiding is a kind of standing still: the verb is the verb to stand, and the earth stands while the sun runs.

The wind goeth toward the south. The Hebrew withholds its subject: going to the south, and turning to the north, turning, turning goes, and only then, the wind. One reads through half the circuit before knowing what is turning. The sun keeps its course from east to west; the wind goes round by north and south, the other two quarters, so that between them the whole compass is traced and nothing is reached. Allegorists saw in its circuits the restlessness of the soul; a sailor sees only the wind, which always comes back.

The eye is not satisfied with seeing. The senses are named as the first of all labourers, and the most tireless. The eye is never full, as the sea is never full; it takes in the world without end and is no fuller afterwards. Montaigne, who had sayings of the Preacher painted on the beams of his library, knew the hunger well, and wrote that our appetite is irresolute and uncertain, able neither to keep a thing nor to enjoy it rightly. The verse does not condemn seeing. It says only that seeing is not arriving.

No new thing under the sun. The most quoted of the Preacher’s sayings, and the most argued with. Every age that believed in its own novelty has had to step around it, and every age has found at last its novelties already catalogued. Yet the sentence is less a law of nature than a confession: it is the voice of one who has tried the new, in gardens and in houses, in singers and in silver, and found each new thing old by the second morning. That is why it is quoted with a smile, and why the smile does not last.

No remembrance of former things. Here is the deeper reason that nothing seems new: not that nothing happens, but that nothing is kept. The generations of the fourth verse return, now without their names. The ancients are forgotten by those who come after them, and those who come after us will be forgotten in their turn. It is the saddest verse of the passage and the most honest; and the page on which it stands, with readers of many centuries gathered round it in the margins, is the one answer to it the Preacher did not foresee.

Hagia Sophia · Constantinople

Gold under glass

A gold tessera is a sandwich. The glassmaker poured out a cake of molten glass, usually with a little green or amber in it, and while it was still warm laid a leaf of gold across it, beaten so thin that a breath would lift it. Over the leaf went a second skin of clear glass, much thinner, which Italian workshops still call the cartellina, the little card: sometimes blown as a bubble and cut open, sometimes poured. The whole was put back in the furnace until the two glasses fused and the gold was sealed between them, safe from the air and from a thousand years of lamp smoke. When it had cooled, the cake was set on a small iron blade fixed in a block, the hardie, and broken with a hammer into cubes about the size of a fingernail. Every cube in the vaults of Ravenna and Constantinople went under that hammer one at a time.

The setters worked a patch at a time, as much wet lime plaster as they could fill before it went hard, over a drawing brushed onto the coat beneath. They pressed each cube in with a thumb, and they did not press them flat. In a gold ground the cubes lean, forward, back, to one side, none quite in line with its neighbour, and the joints between them are left open so that each one meets the light by itself. High in a vault the field was often tipped down toward the floor, where the people stood. None of this survives in a photograph, which is taken in one light from one place. It is a surface made for an eye that moves.

It was lit by oil lamps hung on chains and by candles carried in procession, small flames that moved, and by people who moved under them. Walk the length of the nave with a taper and the wall answers cube by cube: one flares and goes out, the next takes it up, and a spark seems to keep pace beside you through the gold, always just ahead. Procopius, describing Justinian's new church in Constantinople, wrote that you would think the place was not lit from outside by the sun, but that the light was born inside it. He was looking at gold that had been set to do exactly that.

In Hagia Sophia, seven centuries later, the setters laid the ground behind this face in overlapping fans, like the scales of a fish, so that even the empty gold has a grain. Most of these churches face east, and the apse is the first part of them the morning finds. Before sunrise the gold is a dull brown under the lamps. Then a low, level light comes in through the east windows and across the marble floor, and the gold above takes it at a glancing angle from below, the angle the setters tipped it to meet, so that every cube sends a little of the day back down into the church. The night office was timed to end at first light, with the hymn that begins Glory to you who have shown us the light, and its last verses were sung under a vault that was slowly catching. By mid morning the sun is high, the angle has passed, and the gold settles back to a warm and even glow.

On paper

Good writing paper was once made of rags: worn linen shirts and bedsheets, left in damp heaps until they rotted soft, then beaten for hours in a trough of water until the fibres came apart, lifted on a wire mould and pressed between felts. Hold such a sheet to the light and you can still see the mould in it, fine lines close together and stronger ones an inch apart. Rag paper does not flare like newsprint. Put a corner to a flame and it smoulders. The heat runs ahead of the fire and browns the sheet as the cellulose gives up its water; then a thin edge of embers creeps forward, faster along the fibres than across them, and leaves behind a black skin of carbon that shrinks and cracks like glaze. The carbon burns more slowly still, and what is left at the end is ash, the little mineral the fibres held and the chalk the papermaker added: grey, nearly weightless, keeping the shape of the page until a breath lifts it.

Hollins Farm, 3rd October

Dear Nell,

The pears are in, the light goes by six, and the swallows left on Tuesday, all at once, the way they do. It seems the season for saying things.

When you came to help with the lambing that first spring, I learned the sound of your step on the yard stones before I learned your face. I have listened for it ever since. I never said so, because you had Tom, and then the children, and because it never asked for anything. It was only true, and I wanted one other person to know it, once.

Don't answer this. Read it at the kitchen table, put it in the range, and let us go on as we were: the eggs on Fridays, the crossword over the wall.

With my love, which is old and quiet,

Alice

Most letters of this kind were burned, as they asked to be. Cassandra Austen, late in her life, burned the greater part of her sister's letters and cut passages out of others. Dickens made a bonfire of twenty years of his correspondence in the field at Gad's Hill, and his boys roasted onions in the ashes. Others were kept against instructions, in a drawer, under the lining paper, folded in three along creases that went brown. Fire is less thorough than it looks. Iron-gall ink leaves its iron behind when the paper goes, and on the grey ash of a burned page the writing can often still be read, rust on ash, for as long as no one touches it.

An essay on glass

The Colour
on the Floor

On ash and sand, flashed ruby and silver stain, and the light a window lets fall across the stones.

On a clear morning in a church with old glass, the window is only half the work. The other half lies on the floor: a patch of colour thrown down across the stones, ruby and blue and straw-yellow, leaning the way the sun leans and stretched long by its lowness. Close under the wall you can follow the lead lines in it and almost make out a figure. A few paces further out the lines have gone, and the colours have run together into pools, as if someone had spilt them there and walked away.

Ash and sand

The glass began in the forest. Theophilus, a monk who wrote down the crafts of his workshop early in the twelfth century, tells the glassmaker to take two parts of beechwood ash to one of sand, washed clean of earth and stones, and melt them together in a pot of clay. The ash gave potash, which brings the melting point of sand down within reach of a wood fire. It also made a softer, less stable glass than the Romans’ soda glass, which is why the outer face of so many medieval windows is pitted and crusted like the top of a loaf. Colour went into the pot with the batch: cobalt for blue, copper for green, manganese for purple, iron for the yellows and the murky greens, so that each colour runs right through the sheet. Glaziers still call it pot-metal.

A flat sheet started as a bubble. For muff glass the blower swung a long gather into a cylinder, cut off its ends, slit it down one side and laid it back in the furnace to open out flat; for crown glass, later, he spun the bubble on an iron rod until it flashed open into a wide disc, thick in the middle where the rod had been. Either way it came out uneven, full of small seeds of air and streaks where the batch had not quite mixed, and every flaw changes the light that passes through. Red was the hard one. Copper ruby is so dense that a sheet thick enough to handle looks black against the sky, so it was laid on as a skin: a gather of clear glass dipped into the red and blown out with it, clear on one face and flashed with red on the other. Old ruby seen close is dozens of thin layers wavering like the grain of wood, and it throws the deepest colour in the window.

Paint does not colour the light. It stops it.

Iron, paint and lead

The design was drawn full size on a whitewashed table, every piece and every lead line, and the glass laid over it to be cut. There was no diamond to score it. The glazier drew a red-hot iron along the line he wanted, wetted it so the glass cracked after the iron, then nibbled the edge to shape with a hooked grozing iron, which leaves a row of small bites you can still see on old pieces. Faces, hands, folds of cloth and the leaves of the border were painted on in grisaille, a paste of ground glass and iron oxide in wine or gum, and fired in a kiln until it fused into the surface. That is why a saint’s features arrive on the floor as fine brown shadows and not as colour.

Around 1300 someone found that a compound of silver, painted on the back of a clear piece and fired, turned the glass yellow, from the pale of a lemon to a deep orange depending on the heat. Silver stain changed the trade: a golden crown and a white face could now be one piece, with no lead between them, and fourteenth-century windows grew lighter and more silvery. Then every piece was fitted into cames, strips of cast lead grooved on both sides to take the glass, soldered with tin at each joint and braced with iron bars. The heavy black lines that hold a window together are part of its drawing.

It is often said that old windows are thicker at the bottom because glass is a slow liquid that has sagged over the centuries. It isn’t, and it hasn’t. Glass at the temperature of a church is so stiff that it would take far longer than the age of the universe to move a visible distance; Roman cups and old telescope lenses have kept their shapes. Panes are thicker along one edge because muff and crown glass came out uneven, and a sensible glazier set the heavy edge at the bottom. Now and then a piece turns up with its thick edge at the top, set by one who wasn’t.

And then the light moves. A church is built with its altar to the east, so the morning sun comes in through the choir and lays its colours down the length of the nave, long and pale. By noon the sun is high in the south and the patches are short and bright, close under the aisle windows, the blue and the red lying on the stone like rugs. Through the afternoon they lengthen again and swing toward the east, climbing the bases of the piers and folding over the step of a pew, and when a cloud crosses the sun they fade and soften and then come back exactly as they were. Nobody designed that part. The glaziers made the window to be looked at; the floor was a gift.

Before electricity

A Few Feet of Light

For most of history the light people read by was rendered fat. A tallow candle was dipped, layer on layer, from melted mutton or beef suet. It smoked, it smelled of the kitchen, it slumped in a warm room and ran down its own side, and its twisted cotton wick did not burn away but stood up into the flame and charred, so that within a quarter of an hour the light had sunk to a dull and smoking red. Someone had to trim it with snuffers, a pair of scissors with a little box on one blade to catch the hot end; to snuff a candle meant then not to put it out but to cut it back so that it burned bright again. Beeswax needed far less of this. It burned clear and steady, smelled faintly of honey, and cost several times as much, so it went to altars, courts and the rich, and everyone else sat in the smell of tallow.

A candle is a smaller light than we remember. The unit of luminous intensity, the candela, is still roughly one candle’s worth, and it falls away with the square of the distance: a page held a foot from the flame gets about ten lux, at three feet barely one, where an office desk today gets five hundred. So a candle did not light a room so much as a few feet of it. Inside that circle were the hands, the page, a face. Outside it the room went on as a collection of rumours: the glint of a jug, the pale edge of a door, the dark.

Georges de La Tour, working in Lorraine through the war, plague and famine of the 1630s and 40s, painted that circle again and again, and was strangely shy of the flame at its centre. Often he hid it: behind a raised hand whose fingers glow red where the light comes through them, behind a sleeve, a book, the shoulder of a child, so that we see only what it touches. When he did show it, it stands tall and perfectly still, a pale tongue with a dark heart. Here he paints it twice, the candle on the table and its double in the black glass behind, both as motionless as a flame only is in a shut room at night.

Reading by one candle had its own manners. You held the book close and the candle closer, turned the page toward the flame, and kept still, because a candle in still air hardly moves: the hot gas rising off it draws a steady current in at its foot, and that current stands the flame upright and long. It is a draught that makes it flicker. A door in another room, a sleeve passing, someone laughing too near, and the flame lies over and shortens, the light on the page shudders and drops, and if it goes too far a thread of grey smoke climbs from the wick and the room is gone until someone brings a light from the fire.
