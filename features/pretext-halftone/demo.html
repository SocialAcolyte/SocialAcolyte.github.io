He looks down from the top of the dome, from the one place in the church where nobody can stand. The monastery of Daphni sits beside the old Sacred Way from Athens to Eleusis, among pines, on ground where a sanctuary of Apollo once stood. Its church was covered in mosaic around the year 1100, and in the dome the setters put Christ Pantokrator, the ruler of all, as Byzantine churches by then had learned to do. He holds the book of the Gospels against his chest, bound in gold and studded with stones. The halo behind his head carries a cross, and the letters at his shoulders, IC XC, are the first and last letters of his name in Greek. He is not gentle here. Most Pantokrators look out with a calm that is meant to steady whoever looks up; this one frowns, the eyes turned aside, the brows drawn together, the mouth set, and people have argued for a long time over whether he is grieving or judging. The mosaicists worked on the curve of the dome from scaffolding, an arm's length from the face, and they made it to be seen from far below: the eyes larger than life, the lines of the nose and the brow heavy and plain, the shadows laid in rows of dark stones so that from the floor they would read as flesh. Earthquakes have shaken the church more than once. By the end of the nineteenth century much of the mosaic had come loose, and restorers took whole sections down and set them back, stone by stone. Where stones are lost the plaster shows through in pale patches at the edge of the gold, as if the ground had worn thin and the wall were coming up beneath it.

The building is small, a cross of brick hardly bigger than a large room, and from outside it could pass for a storehouse. Galla Placidia, daughter of the emperor Theodosius, sister of one emperor and mother of another, governed the western empire from Ravenna for her young son in the years after 425, and the chapel carries her name, though she most likely died and was buried in Rome. Inside, every wall and vault is mosaic, and the day comes in only through narrow windows of thin alabaster, so the room is dim and blue and gold. At the top of the dome is a sky. Hundreds of gold stars, each with eight points, are set on a deep blue ground in rings that grow smaller toward the summit, so that the vault seems higher than it is, and at the centre of the rings stands a single gold cross. In the four corners below it are the living creatures that came to stand for the evangelists: the man, the lion, the ox and the eagle. The cross is not a crucifix. There is no body on it and no wound. It is the sign in the heavens, the cross that was to appear in the sky at the end of time, and Christians of the fifth century were still slow to show Christ dying. They showed the cross as a victory instead, plain gold among stars, turned toward the east, the side from which the light of the morning comes.
