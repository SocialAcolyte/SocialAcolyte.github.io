Hagia Sophia · Constantinople

Gold under glass

A gold tessera is a sandwich. The glassmaker poured out a cake of molten glass, usually with a little green or amber in it, and while it was still warm laid a leaf of gold across it, beaten so thin that a breath would lift it. Over the leaf went a second skin of clear glass, much thinner, which Italian workshops still call the cartellina, the little card: sometimes blown as a bubble and cut open, sometimes poured. The whole was put back in the furnace until the two glasses fused and the gold was sealed between them, safe from the air and from a thousand years of lamp smoke. When it had cooled, the cake was set on a small iron blade fixed in a block, the hardie, and broken with a hammer into cubes about the size of a fingernail. Every cube in the vaults of Ravenna and Constantinople went under that hammer one at a time.

The setters worked a patch at a time, as much wet lime plaster as they could fill before it went hard, over a drawing brushed onto the coat beneath. They pressed each cube in with a thumb, and they did not press them flat. In a gold ground the cubes lean, forward, back, to one side, none quite in line with its neighbour, and the joints between them are left open so that each one meets the light by itself. High in a vault the field was often tipped down toward the floor, where the people stood. None of this survives in a photograph, which is taken in one light from one place. It is a surface made for an eye that moves.

It was lit by oil lamps hung on chains and by candles carried in procession, small flames that moved, and by people who moved under them. Walk the length of the nave with a taper and the wall answers cube by cube: one flares and goes out, the next takes it up, and a spark seems to keep pace beside you through the gold, always just ahead. Procopius, describing Justinian's new church in Constantinople, wrote that you would think the place was not lit from outside by the sun, but that the light was born inside it. He was looking at gold that had been set to do exactly that.

In Hagia Sophia, seven centuries later, the setters laid the ground behind this face in overlapping fans, like the scales of a fish, so that even the empty gold has a grain. Most of these churches face east, and the apse is the first part of them the morning finds. Before sunrise the gold is a dull brown under the lamps. Then a low, level light comes in through the east windows and across the marble floor, and the gold above takes it at a glancing angle from below, the angle the setters tipped it to meet, so that every cube sends a little of the day back down into the church. The night office was timed to end at first light, with the hymn that begins Glory to you who have shown us the light, and its last verses were sung under a vault that was slowly catching. By mid morning the sun is high, the angle has passed, and the gold settles back to a warm and even glow.
